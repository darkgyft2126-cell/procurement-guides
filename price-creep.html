Price creep

By Michael Olson, Senior Strategic Buyer, 15+ years in manufacturing purchasing.

Short answer: Price creep is a small rise in a part price, repeated over time, with no notice and no agreement. One rise looks too small to fight. The total is not small. You detect it by comparing the invoice price to the agreed price, line by line, on a fixed schedule.

How creep happens

  • The invoice price drifts from the quote price. The quote says $100. The invoice says $101.50. No one flags a $1.50 line.
  • A surcharge appears and stays. A fuel or material surcharge starts as a temporary line. The market falls. The line stays.
  • The annual rise has no cap. The contract allows a rise "per index." No one checks the index math. The rise lands above the index each year.
  • The unit changes. The price per piece stays flat. The pack size drops. The cost per piece rises and the price list looks clean.

Worked example: a quiet 3% annual creep

One part costs $100. You buy 5,000 pieces per year. The price creeps up 3% per year. No one objects, because each step is small.

YearPrice per pieceExtra cost that year
Year 0 (agreed price)$100.00—
Year 1$103.00$15,000
Year 2$106.09$30,450
Year 3$109.27$46,364

The math: each year, price = prior price × 1.03. Extra cost = (new price − $100) × 5,000 pieces.

By year 3, you pay $46,364 more per year for the same part, the same quantity, and the same service. The three-year total of extra cost is $91,814. One part. One supplier. Scale that across a full part list and creep becomes one of the largest silent costs in the plant.

The check to run

Run this check every quarter. It takes one report and one hour.

  1. Pull the price history. For each active part, list the price paid on each invoice for the last 12 months.
  2. Compare to the agreed price. The agreed price is the quote, the contract, or the last approved price. Flag every line where the invoice price is higher.
  3. Flag any rise with no approval. A rise is valid only if you agreed to it in writing, or the contract index allows it and the math is correct.
  4. Claim the difference. Send the supplier the list of flagged lines. Ask for a credit for the overcharge and a reset to the agreed price.
  5. Fix the cause. Set the system to block payment when the invoice price beats the order price past a small tolerance. A three-way match — order, receipt, invoice — stops most creep at the door.

Do the check on a schedule, not on a suspicion. Creep is quiet by design. A schedule is the counter.
